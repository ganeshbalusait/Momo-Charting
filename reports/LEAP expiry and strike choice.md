# Deep, long-dated LEAPs blunt volatility's bite

The practitioner sources point the same way on both questions: for a LEAP call (an ordinary listed call option with more than about a year of life), they favour **the longer expiry, roughly 18 to 24 months, over 12 months**, and **a deep in-the-money strike with a delta of about 0.80 or higher** rather than an at-the-money or out-of-the-money strike. Delta is how many cents the option moves when the stock moves $1; "in the money" means the strike is below today's stock price. In today's market that second point matters more than usual, because option prices are inflated by fear and a deep in-the-money call is the kind least exposed to that inflation leaking back out. On most single stocks the real choice this week is not 12, 18 or 24 months but **January 2028 (about 14 months) or January 2029 (about 26 months)**, because far-dated stock options are listed for January only. Three cautions frame everything below. First, the "0.80 delta" and "buy at least a year" guidance are rules of thumb from educational sources, not tested results; the researchers found no study comparing LEAP deltas or expiries against each other. Second, most of the dollar figures in this report are the researchers' own model calculations on a made-up $100 stock, not market quotes. Third, a deep in-the-money LEAP is not safe: in the model, a further 20% fall in the stock erases 86% to 100% of the premium at expiry. This report is general education on what the evidence says, not advice to buy anything.

## A six-day slide has pushed option prices up, mostly at the short end

The S&P 500 closed Thursday November 19, 2026 at **7,175.83, its sixth straight loss, down 5.1% on the week**, with the Dow below 50,000 and the Nasdaq Composite down 5.0% for the week ([AP via ABC News](https://abcnews.com/Business/wireStory/major-us-stock-indexes-fared-thursday-11192026-138303454)). The VIX, the market's gauge of expected S&P 500 swings over the next 30 days, rose from 24.56 to **about 28.3** in one session; Nvidia closed at $145.98 after six down days, and the 10-year Treasury yield sat near 5.05% with markets fully pricing a Federal Reserve rate rise ([The Weekend Spread](https://theweekendspread.substack.com/p/the-close-thursday-november-19-2026)). One page showed the VIX at 30.73 at an unclear timestamp; the researchers treat that as unconfirmed, and no closing data for November 20 was available ([Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/live/stock-market-today-thursday-november-19-dow-sp-500-nasdaq-anthropic-ipo-live-141306032.html)). The Fed meets December 8-9 ([Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/2026-december.htm)), with a jobs report and an inflation report reported for the days around it by secondary calendars.

This matters for a LEAP buyer because of implied volatility (IV), the amount of future movement baked into an option's price. High IV means expensive options, and a later fall in IV, often called "IV crush", lowers an option's price even if the stock does nothing. One practitioner source puts it bluntly: buy LEAP calls right after a huge move and "you're probably going to overpay" ([Market Rebellion](https://marketrebellion.com/news/thinking-about-trading-leaps-read-this-first)). The sensitivity to IV is called vega, and it is largest for long-dated, at-the-money options ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/concepts/vega)).

There is a mitigating fact. Fear shows up mostly in near-dated options. On November 19 a short-term VIX futures fund rose 12.16% while the mid-term one rose 5.80% ([The Weekend Spread](https://theweekendspread.substack.com/p/the-close-thursday-november-19-2026)), and Cboe notes that near-term volatility trading above longer-term is the unusual state, seen less than 20% of the time since 2010 and usually short-lived ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/inside-volatility-trading-is-vix-backwardation-necessarily-a-sign-of-a-future-down-market)). The researchers infer that a one-to-two-year option carries less "spike premium" than the VIX jump suggests. That is an inference: no data on how much one-to-two-year IV actually rose this month was found. A related trap is that the "IV rank" shown on most platforms, which places current IV between its 52-week low and high ([tastytrade](https://tastytrade.com/learn/platforms-and-tools/research/what-is-iv-rank/)), is usually built from 30-day IV, so it overstates how expensive a LEAP is; the fair check is the IV of the specific expiry.

## Deeper strikes pay for value that cannot melt

An option's price has two parts. Intrinsic value is the amount it is already in the money (stock price minus strike). Time value is everything else, and it is the only part guaranteed to shrink to zero by expiry. The standard educational guidance for using a LEAP call in place of stock is to "look for a delta of .80 or more", starting with a strike at least 20% below the stock price, and to go deeper on volatile stocks ([The Options Playbook](https://optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)). Another source says 70-80 delta ([SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/leaps-options-strategy/)). These are conventions. The reasoning is that the buyer pays mostly for intrinsic value, so the break-even price (strike plus premium paid) sits close to today's price.

The researchers' own model shows the trade-off in numbers. The table below is their Black-Scholes calculation (the standard textbook pricing formula), not a source's figures: a $100 stock, 30% implied volatility, 4% interest, no dividends, no trading costs, 12 months to expiry, per share.

| Strike | Delta | Premium | Time value | Move needed to break even at expiry | Result if stock is flat | Result if stock is +20% | Result if stock is -20% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $80 (deep in the money) | 0.85 | $25.87 | $5.87 | +5.9% | -23% | +55% | -100% |
| $100 (at the money) | 0.61 | $13.75 | $13.75 | +13.8% | -100% | +45% | -100% |
| $135 (out of the money) | 0.24 | $3.62 | $3.62 | +38.6% | -100% | -100% | -100% |

Three things stand out. A flat stock costs the deep in-the-money buyer about a quarter of the premium and costs the other two everything. **A 20% rise, a good year for most stocks, still leaves the 0.24-delta LEAP worthless at expiry.** And on a 20% fall all three lose the full premium, but the deep in-the-money contract loses the most dollars ($2,587 per contract against $362), which is why contract count matters more than the percentage.

A habit from short-dated trading also needs adjusting: delta is often read as the chance of finishing in the money, but that shortcut works less well for longer-dated options ([Macroption](https://macroption.com/delta-calls-puts-probability-expiring-itm)). In the researchers' model the 0.24-delta LEAP has about a 15% chance of finishing in the money, and the at-the-money LEAP (delta 0.61) about 49%. Clearing the break-even is less likely again.

The academic record is the one piece of hard evidence here, and it argues against the out-of-the-money end. A study of US stock options from 1996 to 2005 found that out-of-the-money calls had negative average returns, with one-month calls more than 15% out of the money averaging -28% per holding period, and that the higher the strike the worse the result ([CXO Advisory summary of Ni](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/returns-for-call-options-on-individual-stocks/)). A Journal of Finance paper found that the more lottery-like an option's payoff, the lower its average return ([Boyer and Vorkink](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/jfinan/v69y2014i4p1485-1527.html)). The limit is important: these studies cover mostly short-dated options, were read through abstracts and summaries, and none measured LEAPs. Saying a deep in-the-money LEAP escapes the "lottery" overpricing is a reasoned extension, not a tested result.

High volatility shifts the target strike deeper. In a second model run at 40% IV on two-year calls (again the researchers' own calculation), the strike 20% below the stock reached only 0.79 delta and was still 43% time value; it took a strike 30% below to get to 0.85 delta and 27% time value. The same run shows what IV crush does by strike if the stock stands still and IV falls 10 points: **about -4% for a strike 40% below the stock, -7% at 30% below, -20% at the money, and -40% for a strike 40% above.** The model ignores the fact that real option chains price each strike at a different IV.

## Twenty-four months buys cheaper time, but only near the money

Time decay, or theta, is the daily loss of time value. Sources agree it is slow early and speeds up near expiry ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/concepts-strategies/leaps)). The common practitioner template is to buy "at least a year or more" out ([The Options Playbook](https://optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)) and to close or roll (sell and replace with a later expiry) "with six to twelve months remaining" ([SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/leaps-options-strategy/)). Blogs offer other roll windows, from 3-6 months to 90-120 days, and they conflict; no back-test of any roll rule was found.

The researchers' model (same $100 stock, 30% IV) shows why longer is cheaper per month for at-the-money calls: the 12-month call costs $13.75 and the 24-month call $20.28, so the second year costs $6.53 against $13.75 for the first. Daily decay as a share of premium is about 0.08% at 24 months, 0.15% at 12, 0.30% at 6, 0.59% at 3 and 1.72% at 1 month. There is no single cliff; the bleed roughly doubles each time the remaining life halves.

That picture changes for deep in-the-money calls, and this is the less obvious finding. Veteran options author Lawrence McMillan describes in-the-money time decay as "a rather flat decay - almost linear" ([McMillan](https://optionstrategist.com/node/5141)), and the model agrees: a strike 25% below the stock loses about $0.40 a month at every horizon, so **the longer expiry does not make time cheaper there; it simply buys more months at the same rate.** Part of that cost is interest on the strike not yet paid, which rises with interest rates, and the model's 4% is below today's roughly 5% yields.

So the case for the longer expiry on a deep in-the-money LEAP rests less on theta and more on runway. Past bear markets ran 282 days (2022, -25.4%), 517 days (2007-09, -56.8%) and 929 days (2000-02, -49.1%), while 2020's fall lasted about a month ([Stacker](https://stacker.com/stories/business-economy/every-major-stock-market-correction-1950-and-how-long-recovery-took)). The researchers reason, without sourced option records, that a one-year call bought early in a 2022-style decline would likely have expired at a large loss while a two-year call had time for the recovery, and that neither survived 2000-02 or 2008. As the Playbook says, a rally that starts the day after expiry "does you no good" ([The Options Playbook](https://optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)). Blog-quality data sets suggest stocks do better than average in the year after VIX readings above 30, but in 2008 the market fell roughly another 40% after the first such reading ([eco3min](https://eco3min.fr/en/qa/vix-contrarian-buy-signal/)).

The longer option has costs of its own: more dollars at risk, more sensitivity to IV in dollar terms (model vega of about $38 per contract per IV point at 12 months against $52 at 24), and thinner trading. US tax rules add one fact: a gain on an option held more than one year is long-term ([IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550)), so only a purchase of roughly 18 months or more allows both a one-year hold and a sale with months still left.

## Size, spreads and open interest decide the rest

A deep in-the-money LEAP is sized like stock, not like a lottery ticket. The Playbook's rule is one contract for each 100 shares the buyer would otherwise own, with a profit target and stop-loss set beforehand ([The Options Playbook](https://optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)); McMillan's version is a small part of the money in the call and the rest in Treasury bills ([McMillan](https://www.optionstrategist.com/node/2681)). By the researchers' arithmetic, $10,000 buys 100 shares or nearly four of the model's 12-month $80 calls, which behave like about 330 shares. Percent-of-account limits of 1% to 5% per trade circulate on educational pages, but they are conventions and their attribution was not verified.

LEAPs trade less than near-dated options and the gap between the buying and selling price (the bid-ask spread) is wider ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/concepts-strategies/leaps)). No published figures on typical LEAP spreads were found. On a large premium a modest-looking spread is real money, which is why practitioners use limit orders near the midpoint.

Open interest, the count of contracts outstanding, is not a direction signal. The industry's education body says it "indicates neither a bullish nor bearish outlook" and warns that it does not even prove liquidity; a tight quote does ([OIC](https://www.optionseducation.org/referencelibrary/faq/general-information)). Its sensible use is choosing between neighbouring strikes after the delta is decided.

For entering during a volatility spike, sources describe four devices, each with a cost: a deeper strike (more dollars at risk), a smaller position, buying in pieces over several weeks (the stock may rebound first), and a call debit spread, which means buying one call and selling a higher-strike call. The spread cuts cost and IV exposure but caps the gain, and its exposure to a volatility drop is reduced, not removed ([SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/what-is-iv-crush-options/)). No source offers a reliable rule for timing the purchase.

## Conclusion

The shift from short-dated out-of-the-money calls to LEAPs is less a change of expiry than a change of what is being bought. The familiar trade buys pure time value with a low chance of a large payoff, the corner where the academic evidence shows buyers have lost on average. The LEAP the practitioner sources describe is nearly the opposite: mostly intrinsic value, stock-like movement, a loss capped at a large premium. Buying a far-dated call at a low delta keeps the old trade's odds and adds a higher price and more exposure to today's inflated volatility.

What stays unknown is whether any of this produces better returns. No study or back-test found here compares LEAP calls with short-dated calls or with owning the stock, or tests 0.80 delta, 12 against 24 months, or any roll rule. The guidance rests on how options are priced, which is sound, and on convention, which is untested. The one risk no strike or expiry removes is a decline that is deeper or longer than the option's life.

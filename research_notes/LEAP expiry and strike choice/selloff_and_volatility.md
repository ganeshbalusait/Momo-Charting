## 2. Vega: why long-dated options react more to implied volatility; numbers at 3, 12, 24 months; loss from a 5- or 10-point IV fall

### Takeaway
Vega (the dollar change in an option's price for a 1-point change in implied volatility) grows with time to expiry, so a 2-year call has roughly 2.5-3 times the dollar vega of a 3-month call at the same strike. In percentage terms an at-the-money call loses roughly 10% of its value for a 5-point IV drop and about 20% for a 10-point drop at any of these expiries if IV falls equally across them; a small stock rise does not always cover that.

### Cited Findings
- Vega is highest for at-the-money options with long time to expiry, falls as options go deep in- or out-of-the-money, and approaches zero near expiry; a 90-day ATM option has about 3x the vega of a 10-day ATM option — [FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/concepts/vega)
- LEAPS carry significant vega because a larger part of their premium is time value, which depends on expected future volatility — [Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/strategies/leaps)
- "If you buy call LEAPS immediately following a huge price move, you'll likely be buying when implied volatility is high. You're probably going to overpay" — [Market Rebellion](https://marketrebellion.com/news/thinking-about-trading-leaps-read-this-first)
- Long calls and puts are hurt by an IV collapse because of their vega exposure ("IV crush") — [SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/what-is-iv-crush-options/); see also OIC's "The Crush Is Real" — [Options Industry Council](https://prd-web.optionseducation.org/news/the-crush-is-real) (title found; page not opened)
- One glossary puts LEAPS vega at "often 5 to 10 times" a 30-day option — [Convex Trade](https://convextrade.com/glossary/leaps) (low-authority; consistent in direction with the square-root-of-time rule: sqrt(24 months / 1 month) is about 4.9)

### Inferences
Researcher's own Black-Scholes calculation (not from a source; $100 stock, at-the-money $100 strike, IV 40%, 4% interest rate, no dividends; "vega" = price change per 1 IV point, per share):

| Expiry | Call price | Delta | Vega per 1 pt | Price change if IV 40 -> 35 | if IV 40 -> 30 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 months | $8.43 | 0.56 | $0.20 | -11.7% | -23.4% |
| 12 months | $17.58 | 0.62 | $0.38 | -10.9% | -21.8% |
| 24 months | $25.47 | 0.66 | $0.52 | -10.2% | -20.4% |

- Dollar vega roughly doubles from 3 to 12 months and is about 2.6x at 24 months (it scales with about the square root of time). Per contract (x100): about $20, $38 and $52 per IV point.
- 2-year ATM call bought at IV 40: stock +5% with IV down 5 points gives about +3%; stock +5% with IV down 10 points gives about -7%; stock +10% with IV down 10 points gives about +7%. So a 10-point IV fall can wipe out a 5% stock gain on an ATM LEAP.
- 2-year 20%-out-of-the-money call ($120 strike) bought at IV 40: stock +5% and IV -5 gives about -1%; stock +5% and IV -10 gives about -17%; even stock +10% and IV -10 gives about -2%.
- Important offset (ties to Section 4): in real selloffs far-dated IV usually rises much less than near-dated IV, so a LEAP's IV rarely has 10 points of "spike" to give back; the 3-month option does. Equal IV moves across expiries, as in the table, overstate the LEAP's crush risk relative to the short-dated option.
- Short-dated options lose far more to time decay; the LEAP trades less theta for more vega.

### Gaps
- No exchange/broker (Cboe, OIC, Schwab, Fidelity) table of vega by expiry was retrieved; the numbers above are model outputs under stated assumptions and will differ by stock, rate, dividend and skew.
- No real quote data for how much 1-2 year IV on SPY/QQQ/NVDA rose this month.

## 3. How deep in-the-money LEAPs reduce vega and time-value exposure versus at- or out-of-the-money

### Takeaway
A deep in-the-money LEAP call (delta about 0.80 or higher) is mostly intrinsic value, so a fall in IV removes a small slice of its price; an at- or out-of-the-money LEAP is all time value and takes the full hit. The trade-off is a higher dollar cost per contract and more dollars lost if the stock falls.

### Cited Findings
- For LEAPS as a stock substitute, "look for a delta of .80 or more"; start with a strike at least 20% below the stock price ($80 strike for a $100 stock), deeper for volatile stocks; buy at least a year to expiry; the whole premium can still be lost; size as one call per 100 shares you would normally hold, not more — [The Options Playbook (Ally)](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)
- Deeper in-the-money LEAPS hold more intrinsic value and less time premium, which reduces exposure to a volatility drop; one illustration puts extrinsic value at 20-25% of the premium for a 0.80-delta LEAP — [Options Trading IQ](https://optionstradingiq.com/leap-options/) (blog; single illustration)
- Vega falls as options move deep ITM or OTM — [FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/concepts/vega)
- A 0.80-delta LEAP can drift toward 0.95 delta after a big rally (less leverage), and LEAPS holders do not receive dividends — [MarketXLS](https://marketxls.com/blog/long-term-equity-leaps-options)

### Inferences
Researcher's own Black-Scholes calculation (2-year calls, $100 stock, bought at IV 40%, 4% rate, no dividends):

| Strike | Price | Delta | Vega/pt | Time value | Stock flat, IV -5 | Stock flat, IV -10 | Stock +5%, IV -10 | Stock -20%, IV +10 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $60 (deep ITM) | $47.75 | 0.91 | $0.23 | $7.75 (16%) | -2.3% | -4.1% | +5.8% | -30% |
| $70 | $40.99 | 0.85 | $0.32 | $10.99 (27%) | -3.8% | -7.2% | +3.8% | -30% |
| $80 | $35.05 | 0.79 | $0.40 | $15.05 (43%) | -5.7% | -11.1% | +0.9% | -30% |
| $100 (ATM) | $25.47 | 0.66 | $0.52 | $25.47 (100%) | -10.2% | -20.4% | -7.1% | -29% |
| $120 (OTM) | $18.46 | 0.54 | $0.56 | $18.46 (100%) | -15.2% | -30.5% | -16.7% | -27% |
| $140 (far OTM) | $13.40 | 0.43 | $0.56 | $13.40 (100%) | -20.5% | -40.5% | -26.7% | -23% |

- At high IV (40%), an "80% of stock price" strike only gets delta 0.79 and is still 43% time value; to get under about 25% time value you need the $70 strike or deeper. This matches the Playbook note that volatile stocks need deeper strikes.
- IV-crush damage scales with the share of the price that is time value: about 4% for the $60 strike vs about 20% ATM vs about 40% far OTM for a 10-point IV drop.
- Deep ITM does not protect against a falling stock: a further 20% drop costs about 30% of the option in every row (partly cushioned by rising IV), and the deep ITM contract loses the most dollars ($14.40 vs $3.11 per share for the $140 strike).

### Gaps
- No primary broker source (Schwab/Fidelity) with a vega-by-strike table was retrieved. Model ignores skew (ITM call strikes usually carry higher IV than OTM), dividends, and wide bid-ask spreads on deep ITM LEAPS.

## 4. Timing: buying after a volatility spike vs waiting; IV rank / IV percentile; VIX term structure; scaling in

### Takeaway
Practitioner sources say option buyers are generally better served when IV rank/percentile is low, and that buying right after a big move means paying up for time value. The mitigating fact for LEAPs is the term structure: in a selloff, near-dated implied volatility jumps far more than long-dated, so the "spike premium" in a 1-2 year option is smaller than the VIX move suggests. No source gives a reliable timing rule; staged entry is the common suggestion.

### Cited Findings
- IV rank = where current IV sits between its 52-week low and high; IV percentile = percent of days in the past year IV was below today's level; they can diverge sharply (example: IV rank 17 with IV percentile 91) — [tastytrade](https://tastytrade.com/learn/platforms-and-tools/research/what-is-iv-rank/)
- Higher IV rank readings may favour evaluating premium-selling; lower readings may make premium-buying more attractive; IV percentile 0-30% is a rough sign options are "cheap" — [tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/definitions/implied-volatility-rank)
- "It's best to buy LEAPS when volatility is low" — [Market Rebellion](https://marketrebellion.com/news/thinking-about-trading-leaps-read-this-first)
- VIX futures have been in contango (near below far) more than 80% of the time since 2010 and in backwardation (near above far) less than 20% of the time; backwardation happens when the near term looks more uncertain than the long term, tends to be short-lived, and occurred in 2008, 2011, Q4 2018 and early 2020 — [Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/inside-volatility-trading-is-vix-backwardation-necessarily-a-sign-of-a-future-down-market)
- Cboe's conclusion: outside the biggest selloffs, backwardation does not reliably predict further declines; it "could signal weakness and overall risk in the market but the market can recover quickly", and some of the best market performance follows the biggest selloffs — [Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/inside-volatility-trading-is-vix-backwardation-necessarily-a-sign-of-a-future-down-market)
- A data study of inversions since 2018 concludes the evidence favours reducing position size during backwardation rather than exiting — [FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/vix-term-structure-inversions-since-2018-data-study) (search summary; vendor study)
- Live example, Nov 19, 2026: short-term VIX futures ETF +12.16% vs mid-term +5.80% — [The Weekend Spread](https://theweekendspread.substack.com/p/the-close-thursday-november-19-2026)

### Inferences
- IV rank/percentile is computed from roughly 30-day IV on most platforms; a high reading says short-dated options are expensive, and overstates how expensive a 1-2 year option is. The fair check for a LEAP is the IV of that specific expiry versus its own history.
- Because nobody can tell whether a spike is the top of fear or the start of a larger decline, splitting a planned purchase into several pieces over weeks (some before and some after the Dec 4-10 events, for example) averages both the stock price and the IV paid. This is a risk-spreading device, not an edge.
- Waiting has its own cost: if the stock rebounds quickly, the lower IV does not make up for a higher stock price on a high-delta LEAP (Section 3 table: delta effect dominates vega for deep ITM).

### Gaps
- No Schwab or Fidelity education page was retrieved on IV rank or on timing LEAP purchases; no academic study found that tests "buy LEAP calls after VIX spikes" directly. No tastylive backtest specific to LEAP entries by IV rank was found.
- Cboe's chart of 30-day returns after backwardation had no numbers in the text.

## 5. Historical evidence: forward returns after selloffs / VIX spikes, and the risk the selloff continues (2000-02, 2008, 2020, 2022)

### Takeaway
On average the S&P 500 has done better than usual in the 12 months after VIX readings above 30, but the average hides cases like 2008 where the market fell another 40% after the first spike, and bear markets of 25-57% that lasted 9 months to 2.5 years. A LEAP has an expiry date, so a decline that outlasts it turns a "temporary" loss into a permanent one.

### Cited Findings
- Median 12-month forward S&P 500 return after VIX above 30 since 1990: +22.4% vs +11.3% when VIX is below 15 (price return, one blog dataset); another figure is about +18% vs +10% unconditional — [eco3min](https://eco3min.fr/en/vix-contrarian-almanac-dataset/); [eco3min Q&A](https://eco3min.fr/en/qa/vix-contrarian-buy-signal/) (low-authority, figures differ between its own pages)
- After a VIX close above 40, the next three months were positive only about 67% of the time; the 208 days above 40 cluster in a few episodes and are not independent samples — [ChartingLens](https://chartinglens.com/blog/vix-levels-stock-market-returns) (blog)
- VIX first crossed 30 in September 2008, and the market fell roughly another 40% before bottoming in March 2009; the signal is weakest in credit-driven crises — [eco3min Q&A](https://eco3min.fr/en/qa/vix-contrarian-buy-signal/)
- Leaving the S&P 500 after a volatility spike, even for five days, would have produced net losses versus staying invested in the strategies tested — [Advisor Perspectives](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2024/08/13/leaving-volatile-markets-can-result-missed-upside)
- Bear-market depth and length (S&P 500): 2000-02 -49.1% over 929 days (Mar 24, 2000 to Oct 9, 2002), price recovery took about seven years; 2007-09 -56.8% over 517 days, recovered in 2013; 2020 -33.9% in about a month, recovered in about five months; 2022 -25.4% over 282 days — [Stacker](https://stacker.com/stories/business-economy/every-major-stock-market-correction-1950-and-how-long-recovery-took); [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/slideshow/12-bear-markets-in-history-what-happened-and-how-long-they-lasted)
- The Nasdaq fell more than 75% in 2000-02 and did not pass its 2000 high until 2014 — [Motley Fool](https://www.fool.com/slideshow/12-bear-markets-in-history-what-happened-and-how-long-they-lasted)
- If LEAP calls expire out-of-the-money, "you may lose your entire investment", and a rally that comes after expiry does not help — [The Options Playbook](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)

### Inferences
- Applying those drawdowns to a 2-year call: in 2000-02 and 2007-09 the decline lasted about as long as or longer than a typical LEAP's life and was deep enough to leave even 20-30% in-the-money strikes worthless or nearly so at expiry. In 2020 a 2-year LEAP bought before the fall would have been deeply underwater for weeks and then recovered within its life. In 2022 (282 days down, tech far worse than the index) a 1-year LEAP on a tech name bought early in the decline would likely have expired at a large or total loss, while a 2-year one had time for the 2023 recovery. These are logical implications of the dates and depths, not sourced option P&L.
- The Section 3 model shows the short-run mark-to-market: a further 20% stock fall costs roughly 30% of a 2-year call's value at any strike even with IV rising 10 points.
- "Average" post-spike returns apply to index holders with unlimited time; an option buyer also needs the recovery to arrive before expiry and to exceed the time value paid.

### Gaps
- No academic paper or broker study was retrieved; the forward-return figures come from blogs and should be treated as indicative only. No sourced historical P&L for actual LEAP calls held through 2000-02, 2008, 2020 or 2022 was found.
- A Nasdaq 2000-02 decline figure of "78%" often quoted was not confirmed; sources only say "more than 75%".

## 6. Alternatives beginners are commonly pointed to (described neutrally)

### Takeaway
The commonly cited ways to limit cost or IV risk on a long-dated bullish view are: a call debit spread (buy one call, sell a higher-strike call), a deeper in-the-money strike, a smaller position, and staged entry. Each lowers one risk and gives something up.

### Cited Findings
- Debit spreads are "partially hedged, but net vega is still positive; can lose on a modest IV collapse" — the sold call offsets part of the bought call's vega, not all — [SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/what-is-iv-crush-options/)
- Position size: buy the number of LEAP contracts that matches the shares you would normally own (1 per 100 shares) and avoid oversizing because the full premium is at risk; set a profit target and stop-loss in advance — [The Options Playbook](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options)
- Deep in-the-money (delta 0.80+) LEAPS as a stock substitute carry less time premium — [The Options Playbook](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/rookies-corner/buying-leap-options); [Options Trading IQ](https://optionstradingiq.com/leap-options/)
- Evidence favours reducing size rather than exiting during VIX backwardation — [FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/vix-term-structure-inversions-since-2018-data-study)

### Inferences
- Call debit spread: lower cost and lower vega than a single call; the gain is capped at the short strike, and long-dated spreads gain value slowly until near expiry even when the stock rises. From the Section 3 model, buying the $100 call and selling the $140 call (2-year, IV 40) costs about $12.07 vs $25.47 and has net vega of about -$0.04 per point (close to zero) vs $0.52, at the price of capping the payoff at $40.
- Smaller size is the only alternative that lowers every risk at once (stock, IV, time) without capping gains.
- Owning shares (or fewer shares) has no expiry and no vega; mentioned for completeness as the baseline against which LEAP leverage is judged.
